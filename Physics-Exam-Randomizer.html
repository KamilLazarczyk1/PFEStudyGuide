Charge properties

Three fundamental properties of electric charge:
  • Quantization — charge exists in integer multiples of the elementary charge: $Q = ne$, where $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.
  • Conservation — total charge of an isolated system stays constant.
  • Invariance — the magnitude of a charge does not depend on its velocity / reference frame.
Two kinds exist (+/−); like charges repel, unlike attract.

Electric charge distributions and charge densities (line, surface, volume)

Each density is the local charge per unit length / area / volume (a derivative); the total charge is recovered by integrating the density:
  • Line: $\tau = \dfrac{dQ}{dl}\ \left[\text{C/m}\right] \;\Rightarrow\; Q = \displaystyle\int_L \tau\,dl$
  • Surface: $\sigma = \dfrac{dQ}{dS}\ \left[\text{C/m}^2\right] \;\Rightarrow\; Q = \displaystyle\int_S \sigma\,dS$
  • Volume: $\rho = \dfrac{dQ}{dV}\ \left[\text{C/m}^3\right] \;\Rightarrow\; Q = \displaystyle\int_V \rho\,dV$

Coulomb's law

The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. In vector form — force on $q_2$ due to $q_1$, with $\vec{r}_{12}$ the vector pointing from $q_1$ to $q_2$: $$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_{12}|^3}\,\vec{r}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat{r}_{12}$$ Directed along the line joining them — repulsive for like charges, attractive for unlike.

Electric field intensity — definition and field at distance r from a point charge q

Field intensity = force per unit positive test charge: $$\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}\ \left[\text{V/m} = \text{N/C}\right]$$ For a point charge, in vector form ($\vec{r}$ points from the charge to the field point): $$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{|\vec{r}|^3}\,\vec{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{r}$$

Formula for the work of electric forces

Work to move charge $q$ from $a$ to $b$ equals the drop in potential energy: $$W = q\,(\varphi_a - \varphi_b) = \int_a^b q\,\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = -\Delta U$$ The electrostatic force is conservative, so $W$ is path-independent.

Definition of voltage

Voltage (potential difference) between two points — the work per unit charge to move a charge between them, equal to the line integral of the field: $$U_{ab} = \varphi_a - \varphi_b = \int_a^b \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}\ \left[\text{V}\right]$$ (With this subscript/limit ordering there is no leading minus; the minus lives in the field–potential relation $\vec{E}=-\nabla \varphi$, used in the potential definition.)

Definition of potential; potential of point P at distance R from charge q

Electric potential at $P$ = work per unit charge to bring a positive test charge from infinity to $P$ (against the field). From $\vec{E}=-\nabla \varphi$: $$\varphi = \frac{W_{\infty\to P}}{q_0} = -\int_{\infty}^{P}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}\ \left[\text{V}\right]$$ For a point charge: $$\varphi = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{R}$$

Potential from superposition (point, line, surface, volume distributions)

Add the contribution of every source element:
  • Point charges: $\varphi = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\sum_i \frac{q_i}{r_i}$
  • Line: $\varphi = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\int \frac{\tau\,dl}{r}$
  • Surface: $\varphi = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\int \frac{\sigma\,dS}{r}$
  • Volume: $\varphi = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\int \frac{\rho\,dV}{r}$

Divergence — definition and in Cartesian coordinates

Divergence = net outward flux of the field per unit volume (a source/sink density). Definition: $$\operatorname{div}\vec{E} = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S}}{\Delta V}$$ In Cartesian coordinates: $$\operatorname{div}\vec{E} = \nabla\cdot\vec{E} = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z}$$

Poisson and Laplace equations

  • Poisson (region with charge): $\nabla^2 \varphi = -\dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon}$
  • Laplace (charge-free region, $\rho = 0$): $\nabla^2 \varphi = 0$

Gauss's law — formula and sketch

The net electric flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed charge over permittivity.
  • Integral form: $\displaystyle\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}$
  • Differential form: $\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = \dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$
Field lines from an enclosed charge piercing a closed (Gaussian) surface.

Sketch of electric shielding (shielded cable)

An electric line (cable) is shielded by wrapping the conductor in a (usually grounded) metallic shield: it intercepts external fields and confines the cable's own field, so neighbouring circuits aren't disturbed. Cross-section, centre outwards:
Shielded (coaxial) cable: conductor → dielectric insulation → metallic shield → outer jacket.

List the types of polarisation

Two types of polarisation:
  • Orientational — permanent dipoles align with the applied field (polar materials).
  • Distortion (deformation) — the field induces dipoles by displacing charges: electronic (electron-cloud shift) and ionic (relative ion shift), in non-polar materials.

Definition of electric (dipole) moment

A measure of the separation of equal and opposite charges: $$\vec{p} = q\,\vec{d}\qquad \left[\text{C}\cdot\text{m}\right]$$ Directed from the negative to the positive charge.

Definition of the polarisation vector P

Macroscopic dipole moment per unit volume of the dielectric: $$\vec{P} = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{\sum_i \vec{p}_i}{\Delta V}\ \left[\text{C/m}^2\right]$$

Bound charge formula

Total bound charge enclosed by a closed surface = negative flux of the polarisation $\vec{P}$: $$Q_b = -\oint_S \vec{P}\cdot d\vec{S}\ \ [\text{C}]$$

Classification of dielectrics

Dielectrics are classified by how their permittivity $\varepsilon$ behaves:
  • Homogeneous vs inhomogeneous — does $\varepsilon$ depend on position $(x,y,z)$?
  • Linear vs non-linear — does $\varepsilon$ depend on the field $\vec{E}$? (non-linear e.g. ferroelectrics)
  • Isotropic vs anisotropic — does $\varepsilon$ depend on direction?
  • Dispersive — does $\varepsilon$ depend on frequency?
Also described by dielectric loss and breakdown voltage. (By dipole type: polar, e.g. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, vs non-polar, e.g. $\text{O}_2$.)

Boundary condition for vector D

The normal component of $\vec{D}$ jumps by the free surface charge density: $$D_{1n} - D_{2n} = \sigma_s$$ If there is no free surface charge ($\sigma_s = 0$): $\;D_{1n} = D_{2n}$ (normal component continuous).

Capacitance — parallel-plate, cylindrical, spherical

  • Parallel plate: $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon A}{d}$
  • Cylindrical (length $L$, radii $a<b$): $C = \dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon L}{\ln(b/a)}$
  • Spherical (radii $a<b$): $C = 4\pi\varepsilon\,\dfrac{ab}{b-a}$

Poynting's hypothesis

Poynting's hypothesis — electromagnetic energy is stored in the field, described by an energy density. Electric part: $$w_e = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec{E}\cdot\vec{D} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r E^2\ \left[\text{J/m}^3\right]$$ Magnetic part: $w_m = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{H}$. Total energy stored in a volume: $$W = \int_V \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec{E}\cdot\vec{D} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{H}\right) dV$$

Examples of dielectrics with relative permittivity

  • Vacuum: $\varepsilon_r = 1$
  • Air: $\varepsilon_r \approx 1.0006$
  • Paper: $\varepsilon_r \approx 3.7$
  • Glass: $\varepsilon_r \approx 5\text{–}10$
  • Pure water: $\varepsilon_r \approx 80$

Electric current — definition, characteristics, relation to field and potential

Current = net rate of charge flow through a cross-section: $$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}\ \left[\text{A}\right]$$ Conventional current flows from high to low potential (direction of positive carriers), driven by the internal field via $\vec{J} = \sigma\vec{E}$. It is a scalar; the field $\vec{E}$ inside the conductor is non-zero.
Field $\vec E$ drives current $I$ from + to − terminal through the conductor.

Volume, surface and linear current density definitions

  • Volume current density: $\vec{J} = \dfrac{dI}{dS_\perp}\ \left[\text{A/m}^2\right]$
  • Surface current density: $\vec{K} = \dfrac{dI}{dl_\perp}\ \left[\text{A/m}\right]$
  • Linear (line) current: $I = \dfrac{dQ}{dt}\ \left[\text{A}\right]$

Current intensity

A scalar = total charge crossing a cross-section per unit time; the flux of $\vec{J}$ through a surface: $$I = \int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}\ \left[\text{A}\right]$$

Continuity equation (integral and differential) + meaning

  • Differential form: $\nabla\cdot\vec{J} = -\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$
  • Integral form: $\displaystyle\oint_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S} = -\frac{dQ}{dt}$
Expresses conservation of charge: the net current leaving a closed surface equals the rate at which the enclosed charge decreases. (Steady state $\Rightarrow \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$.)
Current flowing out of a closed volume ⇒ charge inside is decreasing.

Boundary condition for Jn

For steady currents, the normal component of current density is continuous across an interface (no charge accumulation): $$J_{1n} = J_{2n}$$

Ohm's law — point form and circuital form

  • Point (local) form: $\vec{J} = \sigma\vec{E}$  (current density = conductivity × field)
  • Circuital form: $U = IR$

Resistivity of copper (with unit)

$$\rho_{Cu} \approx 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m} \;\approx\; 0.0175\ \frac{\Omega\cdot\text{mm}^2}{\text{m}}\quad(\text{at }20^\circ\text{C})$$ One of the best common conductors (very low resistivity).

Temperature dependence of resistance — formula and α values

$$R(T) = R_0\,\bigl[\,1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)\,\bigr]$$ $\alpha$ = temperature coefficient of resistance:
  • Copper: $\alpha_{Cu} \approx 4\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
  • Metals: $\alpha \approx (4\text{–}5)\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}\,(>0)$ — resistance rises with temperature.
  • Electrolytes: $\alpha \approx -(1.5\text{–}2.5)\times10^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-1}\,(<0)$ — resistance falls with temperature.
  • Semiconductors: $\alpha \approx -(10^{-2}\text{–}10^{-1})\ \text{K}^{-1}\,(<0)$.

EMF (electromotive force) definition

Energy supplied by a source (e.g. a battery) per unit charge to drive current around a complete circuit: $$\varepsilon = \frac{W}{q} = \oint \vec{E}_{\text{ext}}\cdot d\vec{l}\ \left[\text{V}\right]$$

Kirchhoff's first law (KCL) and its physical basis

The sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it: $$\sum I = 0 \quad\text{(at a node)}$$ Results from conservation of electric charge.

Kirchhoff's second law (KVL) and its physical basis

The algebraic sum of EMFs and voltage drops around any closed loop is zero: $$\sum \varepsilon = \sum IR \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum U = 0$$ Results from conservation of energy.

Joule heating — point and circuital form

  • Circuital form: $P = I^2 R = UI = \dfrac{U^2}{R}\ \left[\text{W}\right]$
  • Point form (power density): $w = \vec{J}\cdot\vec{E} = \sigma E^2\ \left[\text{W/m}^3\right]$

Safety standards for DC and AC current

Protective grounding (earthing), plus a limit on the current through the body. Maximum permissible body current: $$I_{DC} \le 50\,\text{mA}\quad(\text{DC}),\qquad I_{AC} \le 25\,\text{mA}\quad(\text{AC})$$ AC at mains frequency is more dangerous, so its limit is lower.

Grassmann law (force on a current element)

Grassmann's law gives the magnetic force between two current elements $I_1\,d\vec{l}_1$ and $I_2\,d\vec{l}_2$: $$d\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I_1 I_2}{|\vec{r}_{12}|^3}\,\bigl[\,d\vec{l}_2 \times (d\vec{l}_1 \times \vec{r}_{12})\,\bigr]$$ As a special case, the force on a single current element in an external field $\vec{B}$: $$d\vec{F} = I\,d\vec{l}\times\vec{B}$$

Biot–Savart law

Magnetic flux density produced by a current element: $$d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}$$

Boundary condition for vector B

The normal component of $\vec{B}$ is always continuous across a boundary (no magnetic monopoles, $\nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0$): $$B_{1n} = B_{2n}$$

Definition of H (magnetic field intensity)

$\vec{H}$ separates the effect of free (macroscopic) currents from bound material currents: $$\vec{H} = \frac{\vec{B}}{\mu_0} - \vec{M} = \frac{\vec{B}}{\mu}\ \left[\text{A/m}\right]$$ where $\vec{M}$ is the magnetization.

Ampère's law

The line integral of $\vec{H}$ around a closed loop equals the enclosed free current.
  • Integral form: $\displaystyle\oint \vec{H}\cdot d\vec{l} = I_{enc}$
  • Differential form: $\nabla\times\vec{H} = \vec{J}$

Maxwell–Faraday law

A time-varying magnetic field produces a circulating (non-conservative) electric field.
  • Integral form: $\varepsilon = -\dfrac{d\Phi_m}{dt}$
  • Differential form: $\nabla\times\vec{E} = -\dfrac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}$

Mutual induction — definition

The production of an EMF in one coil/circuit caused by a time-varying current in a nearby second coil/circuit: $$\varepsilon_2 = -M\,\frac{dI_1}{dt}$$ where $M$ is the mutual inductance.

EMF of self- and mutual induction

  • Self-induction: $\varepsilon_L = -L\,\dfrac{dI}{dt}$
  • Mutual induction: $\varepsilon_M = -M\,\dfrac{dI_2}{dt}$

Magnetic energy of an inductance carrying current

Total energy stored in the magnetic field of an inductor: $$W_m = \frac{1}{2}L I^2$$

Density of magnetic field energy

Magnetic energy stored per unit volume: $$w_m = \frac{1}{2}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{H} = \frac{1}{2}\mu H^2 = \frac{B^2}{2\mu}\ \left[\text{J/m}^3\right]$$

Maxwell's equations in free space (no charges/currents), point form

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = 0 \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec{H} = 0$$ $$\nabla\times\vec{E} = -\mu_0\frac{\partial\vec{H}}{\partial t} \qquad \nabla\times\vec{H} = \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}$$

Helmholtz wave equation

The frequency-domain wave equation derived from Maxwell's equations: $$\nabla^2\vec{E} + \beta^2\vec{E} = 0,\qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$$ where $\beta$ is the phase constant (wave number).

Velocity of an EM wave in free space

$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} \approx 3\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$$

Drawing of a uniform plane wave

$\vec{E}$ and $\vec{H}$ are mutually perpendicular and both perpendicular to the direction of propagation $\vec{k}$ (a transverse wave); they oscillate in phase.
$\vec E \perp \vec H \perp \vec k$ — fields in phase, propagating along $\vec k$.

Wave impedance of free space

Ratio of the $E$ and $H$ field magnitudes in free space: $$\eta_0 = \frac{E}{H} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} \approx 120\pi \approx 377\ \Omega$$

Depth of penetration (skin depth)

The distance a wave travels into a conductor before its amplitude drops to $1/e$ ($\approx 37\%$): $$\delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}\ \left[\text{m}\right]$$ Higher frequency / conductivity ⇒ smaller skin depth.
